Every project reads the same source now.

Until this summer, each of my Claude projects kept its own copy of what it knew. Now they all read from one set of Notion databases, and Claude keeps those databases current so I don't have to.

Before

Every project had its own copy, and the copies disagreed.

Each Claude project had its own knowledge files: markdown I wrote, uploaded, and was supposed to keep current. The same fact lived in three or four places. When something changed, I had to remember every project that knew it, and I usually didn't. One project would know something different about a topic than the next one, and each answered confidently from whatever version it had.

Putting those facts in my personal preferences wasn't the answer either. Preferences load on every message, in every project, so anything in there gets applied where nobody asked for it (the same cast-iron-and-Destiny problem from my Gemini days, told on my AI practice page).

I'd also been here before with Notion itself. I used it years ago and spent hours building and maintaining databases by hand. Then I stopped, and all of it went stale. I still have pages of it to clear out.

What changed

Claude writes to Notion. I don't.

In late July and early August 2026, I stood up a new project knowing I didn't want to keep updating markdown files in it. So instead of attaching knowledge files, I had Claude design databases in Notion and point the project at them. Then I did the same for the next project, and the one after that.

What makes it work is that I'm not the one maintaining it. When something changes, I say so in conversation and Claude updates the row. Claude reads from it, builds a new database when a project needs one, and marks a page as history when its job is done instead of deleting it. The rules for how it writes live on the hub pages themselves: check for a duplicate before creating a row, update a row instead of adding a second one, and tag the person instead of typing their name.

Because Claude designed the databases, they're related in ways I never bothered to build by hand. People is the spine. Trips, celebrations, activities, and more all tag against it, so a person flows through everything connected to them, and Claude can follow that thread across all of it.

Recipes were the proof. They used to live in a separate app, with a Gemini gem that forgot every good dish the moment the chat ended. Now they're a Notion database. My Digital Sous Chef project pulls from it, and when we build a new recipe together and I like it, I ask and it goes into the library as a new recipe. I don't lift a finger.

The map

What's in it, and how it connects.

Two hubs, a manifest, and the databases hanging off them. This is the version I'm comfortable showing. The parts that hold money, health, and my kids' day-to-day stay off this page.

The manifest is the part most people would skip. It's one database listing every Claude project and skill I run, what each one owns, and which Notion pages it reads, with a separate queue for ideas that haven't been built yet. When I need to know whether something exists, I ask the manifest instead of my memory.

One source still drifts if nobody checks it. While I was building this page, Claude read the household hub and found it still describing Digital Sous Chef as an idea that hadn't been built, while the manifest had it as active. It took one edit in one place to fix. Before, I'd have had to hunt down every project that believed the old version.

Permissioning

Two hubs, because not every project should read everything.

The first version was one shared hub. It holds household logistics – schedules, trips, subscriptions, celebrations – and my wife and I share it, along with every household project. Then I started putting career material in Notion, and some of it had no business sitting where every household project could read it. So there's a second, private hub for professional work and anything else that's mine alone.

The honest limit is written on the private hub itself. The Notion connector reads whatever my account can see, with no per-project scoping. Putting a page on the private hub keeps it away from other people, and it does nothing to keep it away from other Claude projects. Anything that truly can't be read everywhere needs real Notion permissions, not a different parent page.

It's the same lesson as the permissioning work on my AI practice page: what the tool can reach decides what you can build, so find that out before you design around it.

The same pattern at work

At Henry Schein One, the same idea runs on SharePoint.

I pitched Notion as the CS Ops knowledge system and lost, so I built it in SharePoint with licenses the org already had. The SOPs sat in an Excel list that only premium Copilot users could search, inside an ungoverned document library. I restructured the CS SharePoint site into governed libraries (enablement guides, infographics, and Office files in one, team call recordings in another) plus a list of external-facing product links, and kept the structure flat with heavy metadata.

The agent on top of it is grounded only in those sources. A CSM asks Copilot a question and gets leadership-approved steps with a link to the source document, or a content request form when no SOP exists yet. It can't see account data, so CSMs paste that in by hand, and that part still feels half rolled out.

The two builds teach each other. What I learn at home about keeping one source current shows up at work, and what work forces on me – governance, permissions, a form for the gaps – comes back home.

What this gives me back.

This week I listened to an episode of Unchurned, with Josh Schachter at Notion's New York office talking to Christina Parra, Ariel Risman, and Kellie Woodin from Notion's Outcomes Architects team. It described what I've been building this summer almost step for step.

Christina walked through the maturity model they use with customers: AI as a thought partner, then a personal assistant working from your context, then a teammate, and finally AI as the system. She also talked about using Notion to handle the administrative load at home so she can be more present with her family.

That's the arc on my AI practice page, and this knowledge base is how I got from the third step to the fourth. Claude took over the admin work of keeping it all straight. I keep the judgment, and I get more time with my family.
